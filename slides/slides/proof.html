The gate caught its first card

Type 2 diabetes mellitus. Twenty-six claims checked, all seven cited FDA label PDFs opened rather than trusted.

What the card saidWhat the label actually says
Metformin, with a link to its FDA labelThat link resolves to a combination product of two drugs. A clinician clicking it lands on the wrong label.
Boxed warning lists renal impairment, sepsis, alcoholThe label also lists age 65 and over, and imaging with contrast. The contrast hold is a routine decision the card would not have prompted.
A kidney indication marked unverifiedIt is approved, and documented in the very label the card cites. The hedge became its own misinformation.

Verdict: BLOCK

Release is unavailable to the reviewer until these are corrected. Three defects, ten further fixes, on a card that read well.

Informational reference. Not clinical decision support; not for the care of an individual patient.

This is the slide to spend time on. It is the difference between a control and a claim about a control. Every one of these three got past a research agent and a card builder. The fact-checker found them by opening the source documents, which is the whole design: a real link attached to a claim it does not support is invisible to anyone who does not click. The third one is the most instructive. The research could not reach a primary source, so the card hedged - and the hedge told a provider that an FDA-approved renal indication was dubious. Hedging is not automatically safe. If asked "so the system produces bad cards?" - it produced one draft with three defects and then refused to release it. That is the system working. The alternative is the same draft with no check. If asked how often: one card is not a rate. Measuring the real block rate over the first two waves is on the last slide.
